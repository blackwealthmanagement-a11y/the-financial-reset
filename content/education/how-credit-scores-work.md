# How Credit Scores Work

## Introduction

Your credit score is one of the most important numbers in your financial life. It influences whether you're approved for loans, the interest rates you receive, apartment applications, insurance premiums, and sometimes even employment opportunities.

Despite its importance, many people don't actually understand how a credit score is calculated. There are countless myths online—some believe checking your own credit hurts your score, while others think carrying a balance every month improves it. Both are false.

The good news is that credit scores are built from predictable habits. Once you understand the factors involved, you'll know exactly where to focus your attention.

This lesson will explain what a credit score is, how it's calculated, what causes it to increase or decrease, and the practical habits that lead to long-term improvement.

---

# What Is a Credit Score?

A credit score is a numerical estimate of how likely you are to repay borrowed money.

Lenders use this score to help determine risk before approving applications for credit cards, mortgages, auto loans, personal loans, and other financing.

Although there are multiple scoring models, most scores fall between **300 and 850**.

Generally speaking:

- 300–579: Poor
- 580–669: Fair
- 670–739: Good
- 740–799: Very Good
- 800–850: Excellent

Higher scores generally lead to lower interest rates, better approval odds, and more financial options.

---

# The Five Main Credit Score Factors

Although different scoring models weigh information differently, most credit scores are influenced by five primary categories.

## 1. Payment History

Payment history is the single most important factor.

Lenders want evidence that you consistently pay your bills on time.

Late payments, collections, charge-offs, and defaults can negatively affect your score for years.

Positive habits include:

- Paying every account before the due date
- Setting automatic payments
- Making at least the minimum payment if money is tight

Consistency matters more than perfection.

---

## 2. Credit Utilization

Credit utilization measures how much of your available revolving credit you're currently using.

Example:

Credit Card Limit: $5,000

Current Balance: $1,500

Utilization:

$1,500 ÷ $5,000 = 30%

Lower utilization generally indicates responsible credit management.

Many financial professionals recommend staying below **30%**, while keeping balances under **10%** may provide even stronger results.

---

## 3. Length of Credit History

Older accounts provide more information about your borrowing habits.

Closing your oldest credit card isn't always the best decision, especially if it has no annual fee.

Long-established accounts often strengthen your overall credit profile.

---

## 4. New Credit

Every time you apply for certain types of financing, a lender may perform a hard inquiry.

One inquiry usually has only a small temporary effect.

However, multiple applications within a short period may indicate increased borrowing risk.

Only apply for credit when there's a genuine need.

---

## 5. Credit Mix

Credit scores also consider the variety of credit accounts you manage.

Examples include:

- Credit Cards
- Auto Loans
- Student Loans
- Personal Loans
- Mortgages

A healthy mix may help over time, but you should never open unnecessary accounts solely to improve your score.

Responsible management is always more important than account variety.

---

# What Doesn't Affect Your Credit Score?

Many people waste time worrying about things that don't directly impact their score.

Examples include:

- Your income
- Your bank account balance
- Your job title
- Your race
- Your age (once you're eligible for credit)
- Your education level

These factors may influence lending decisions, but they are not direct components of most credit scoring models.

---

# Common Credit Score Myths

## Myth: Checking your own credit hurts your score.

False.

Checking your own credit is considered a soft inquiry and does not reduce your score.

---

## Myth: Carrying a balance improves your credit.

False.

You do not need to pay interest to build credit.

Paying your statement balance in full each month is often the healthiest long-term strategy.

---

## Myth: Closing a paid-off credit card always helps.

Not necessarily.

Closing an account may reduce your available credit and increase your utilization ratio.

Consider the long-term effects before closing older accounts.

---

## Myth: One late payment ruins your credit forever.

No.

Although late payments can significantly impact your score, positive payment history over time can help rebuild your credit profile.

Recovery is possible through consistent financial habits.

---

# Practical Example

Imagine two individuals.

### Person A

- Pays every bill on time
- Uses 15% of available credit
- Has three credit cards open for several years
- Applies for new credit only when necessary

Person A is likely to maintain a strong credit profile.

---

### Person B

- Frequently misses payments
- Maxes out multiple credit cards
- Opens several new accounts in one month
- Ignores collections

Person B presents more risk to lenders and will likely have a lower credit score.

The difference isn't income.

It's financial behavior over time.

---

# How to Improve Your Credit Score

Improving credit rarely happens overnight.

Instead, focus on consistent habits.

Start by:

- Paying every bill on time
- Lowering credit card balances
- Avoiding unnecessary applications
- Reviewing your credit reports regularly
- Correcting inaccurate information
- Keeping older accounts open when appropriate

Small improvements repeated over many months often produce meaningful long-term results.

---

# Frequently Asked Questions

## How often do credit scores update?

Most credit scores update after lenders report new account information, often once each month.

---

## Is an 850 score necessary?

No.

Many lenders already consider scores above approximately 760 to represent excellent credit.

---

## Can I build credit without debt?

Yes.

Using a credit card responsibly and paying the balance in full each month allows you to establish positive payment history without carrying unnecessary debt.

---

## How long does negative information remain?

Different types of negative information remain for different periods.

Late payments, collections, and other derogatory marks may remain on your credit reports for several years depending on the reporting rules.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Credit scores measure borrowing risk.
- Payment history has the greatest impact.
- Lower credit utilization is generally better.
- Long credit history can strengthen your profile.
- Too many applications may temporarily lower your score.
- Good credit is built through consistent habits—not quick fixes.

---

# Action Steps

1. Review your current credit score.
2. Check your utilization on every credit card.
3. Set automatic payment reminders.
4. Pay all future bills on time.
5. Review your credit reports for accuracy.
6. Create a plan to reduce revolving balances.

---

# Conclusion

Your credit score isn't determined by luck.

It's the result of financial decisions repeated over time.

Every on-time payment, every responsible borrowing decision, and every reduction in debt helps strengthen your financial profile.

The goal isn't simply to achieve a higher number.

The goal is to build financial habits that create lasting opportunities for years to come.
